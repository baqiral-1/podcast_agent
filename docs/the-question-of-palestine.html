1 The Art of Not Seeing

The episode opens with Bentwich's 1897 pilgrimage—a Victorian Zionist who sees ancient glory through train windows but cannot see the Palestinian villages surrounding him—establishing the cognitive mechanism that makes dispossession conceivable. It then follows Herzl's extraordinary exchange with Yusuf Diya, where a former mayor of Jerusalem writes an informed, respectful warning and receives a reply that either deliberately deceives or reveals genuine inability to register Palestinian political existence. The Balfour Declaration crystallizes this non-recognition into diplomatic architecture: 'national home' rather than 'state,' 'non-Jewish communities' rather than naming the 93% Arab majority. Britain's contradictory commitments—McMahon, Sykes-Picot, Balfour—create structurally irreconcilable claims. The episode then shifts to the institutional consequences: the Mandate recognizes the Jewish Agency as a quasi-governmental body while Palestinians are denied equivalent standing. The Yishuv builds a complete para-state—Histadrut labor union, Hebrew school system, Haganah military, intelligence networks, taxation—all under imperial protection. The 1936-39 Arab Revolt demonstrates the power and limits of Palestinian mobilization: Britain crushes it using massive military force, destroying leadership cadres and fragmenting social cohesion. The 1939 White Paper reverses course but comes too late; Palestinian institutional capacity is shattered. The episode ends with the decisive gap: by 1947, one side has a state-in-waiting; the other has been systematically unmade.
