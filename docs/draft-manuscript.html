The Iranian Revolution
Seven episodes. Ten books.
Drawn from 10 works of history, politics, and reportage

The series opens by establishing the deep structural foundations — the thousand-year duel between clergy and crown, the Safavid settlement, the Usuli triumph — that made clerical revolutionary leadership possible. It then traces how the Pahlavi modernization project systematically created every condition necessary for its own destruction.

“the thousand-year duel between clergy and crown”

The middle episodes reveal the invisible revolution — the intellectual currents from gharbzadegi through Shariati to Khomeini’s erfan, the organizational networks from the bazaar-mosque alliance to cassette sermons — that was building beneath the surface while both the Shah and America remained blind. The climactic episodes trace the revolutionary year itself and the revolution’s devouring of its children, arriving at the ultimate question: was this tragedy inevitable, or could it have been otherwise?

Episode 01

The Thousand-Year Duel

1:38:29
The episode establishes the series’ global question by tracing the deep structural foundations of Shi’ite clerical autonomy. Beginning with the Safavid imposition of Shi’ism in 1501 and the creation of a clerical class both dependent on and rival to royal power, the episode moves through the Usuli triumph over the Akhbaris, the Qajar dualism that represented a stable equilibrium, and the Constitutional Revolution of 1906 that first revealed the fault line between parliamentary sovereignty and Islamic law. The episode culminates with the paradox of dispossession: Reza Shah’s systematic stripping of clerical prerogatives paradoxically strengthened rather than weakened the ulama’s revolutionary capacity.
“centuries of institutional development created the preconditions for revolution”

The Modernizer’s Trap

1:56:00
The episode traces the self-defeating logic of Pahlavi modernization, showing how father and son pursued nearly identical paradigms — military centralization, secular reform, clerical marginalization — that simultaneously transformed Iran and generated the resentments that fueled opposition. The Pahlavi state was engineering its own destruction through every modernizing act.

The Crucible

1:47:41
The revolution’s crucible and legacy: the Iran-Iraq War as the forge of the Islamic Republic, the 1988 mass executions as the revolution’s moral limit, Montazeri’s dissent as the foreclosure of internal reform, the Sunni-Shia divide as geopolitical fault line. Was this tragedy inevitable or contingent? Both are true simultaneously: structural forces made revolution probable, but its specific form — theocratic rather than democratic — was contingent on personalities, timing, and compounding errors.
